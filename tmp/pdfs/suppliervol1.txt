In 2025, videos of Chinese factories claiming "we make Lululemon / Hermès / Dyson — buy direct from us at
90% off" flooded TikTok. The wave was so big it briefly pushed the wholesale app DHgate to #2 on the US
App Store. There was just one problem: fact-checkers found that most of the viral "brand factories" were
nowhere to be found on the brands' actual supplier lists. They were trading companies borrowing famous
names.

Here's what almost nobody told you: many global brands publish their real supplier lists themselves.
Lululemon posts a facility-by-facility list twice a year. Nike runs a public manufacturing map. Chinese
factories listed on stock exchanges must disclose major clients in their filings.



     How this was actually put together
     Here's the uncomfortable truth about "trusted supplier lists": almost all of them are guesses, or worse —
     trading companies paying to look like factories.

     We took a slower route. Over several weeks we worked through brand-published supplier lists, stock-
     exchange filings and IPO prospectuses from listed Chinese manufacturers, official supplier awards, and
     years of Chinese-language business reporting that never gets read outside China. Each factory was then
     cross-checked against a second independent source before it earned a place in this book.

     None of this is secret. It's all public — if you know Lululemon posts a facility list twice a year, can find a stock
     filing in Mandarin, and can read the line where a manufacturer names Stanley as a client. That's the hard
     part. That's the part we did for you.



Every factory in this directory carries a source tag telling you exactly how we know:



     How every claim is verified
       OFFICIAL      The relationship is disclosed by the brand or the factory itself: a published supplier list, a stock-
     exchange filing / IPO prospectus, or an official supplier award.

       REPORTED        Documented by major business media or securities research (21st Century Business Herald,
     CNBC, SCMP, 36Kr, broker reports). Solid, but one step removed.

     If a claim couldn't clear one of these two bars, it didn't make the book. That's it. That's the methodology.

     Sourcing 101 — the five terms you need
        • OEM (Original Equipment Manufacturer): you bring the design, the factory builds it.
        • ODM (Original Design Manufacturer): the factory already has a designed product; you customize and put
          your logo on it. Fastest, cheapest way to launch.
        • MOQ (Minimum Order Quantity): the smallest order a factory accepts. Negotiable more often than you
          think — especially on existing ODM molds.
        • Tier 1 supplier: the factory that assembles the final product for a brand (vs. Tier 2, which supplies
          materials to Tier 1).
        • Factory vs. trading company: a factory makes things; a trader resells a factory's output with a markup.
          Traders aren't evil — for small first orders a good one can help — but you must know which one you're
          talking to. Page: The Playbook.

     The real answer for viral toys: hunt the cluster, not the "brand factory"
     Sensory fish, dancing cactuses, baby-soothe plush hands — these have no famous brand behind them, so
     anyone claiming to be "the official factory" is marketing. They're born in two clusters: Chenghai, Shantou
     (electronic & plastic toys — thousands of factories, MOQs often 100–500 units) and Yiwu, Zhejiang (plush &
     novelty, plus the world's largest small-commodity market for instant stock).

       1. Screenshot the viral product from the video you saw.
       2. Image-search it on 1688 (camera icon in the search bar — works scarily well). You'll surface dozens of
          makers with prices, usually 5–15% of the TikTok Shop retail price.
       3. Filter for 实力工厂 (verified factory) tags, 3+ years on platform, and Chenghai/Yiwu locations.

       4. Safety first: for anything touching children, demand EN71 (EU) or ASTM F963 / CPC (US) test reports before
          you wire a cent. Real factories have them; traders stall.

     Reality check — the realistic electronics path for a small brand
     Consumer electronics is the one category where "find the brand's factory" genuinely doesn't work for small
     buyers: certified assembly lines cost too much to interrupt. The workable route instead:

        • Shenzhen's ODM layer. Beneath the giants sit thousands of ODMs selling ready-engineered earbuds,
          chargers, trackers and smart gadgets you can brand — MOQs from 500–1,000 units. Global Sources
          Consumer Electronics (HK, April & October) is the show built exactly for this.
        • Certifications are non-negotiable: FCC (US) / CE-RED (EU), and UN38.3 for anything with a lithium
          battery. A supplier who can't produce them instantly is a hard no — batteries are where cheap sourcing
          becomes a house fire.
        • Anker's open secret: the most successful Chinese electronics brand of the past decade doesn't own
          factories — it designs in-house and contracts out. Own the design and QC, rent the manufacturing.

     Five questions that unmask a trading company
       1. "Can we do a live video walk of your production line — today?" Factories say yes; traders schedule, stall,
          or show a suspiciously quiet floor.
       2. "What's your business license scope?" Ask for the license (营业执照); if the scope says 贸易 / 商贸 (trading)
          with no 生产 / 制造 (production), it's a trader.
       3. "Which machines do you run, and how many?" Real factories answer in模具-and-tonnage detail
          instantly; traders get vague.
       4. "Can I see the test report with your company's name on it?" Borrowed certificates carry someone else's
          name. Match the name to the license.
       5. "What DON'T you make?" A factory has a narrow answer ("we only do stainless drinkware"). A trader can
          magically make everything.

     A good trader with real factory relationships can still be worth it for a small first order — just never pay
     factory-direct prices for trader-level service.




     First-order survival rules
        • Sample first, always. $50–150 well spent; factories usually credit it against the bulk order.
        • Payment: 30% deposit / 70% after inspection, before shipment. Never 100% up front. On Alibaba, keep it
          inside Trade Assurance.
        • Third-party inspection (~$100–300): QIMA, SGS or V-Trust checks your goods at the factory before the
          balance is paid. The single highest-ROI line item in importing.
        • Use a freight forwarder for anything beyond a few cartons; they handle customs, duties and the
          alphabet soup (FOB is the incoterm you want as a beginner).
        • Confirm compliance per category: toys EN71/ASTM F963 · electronics FCC/CE + UN38.3 · cosmetics FDA-
          MoCRA/CPNP · anything food-contact LFGB/FDA food-grade.
